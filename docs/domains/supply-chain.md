# Supply chain

A multi-tier supplier network, the orders, shipments, invoices and deliveries that run on it, and **labelled procurement patterns** with legitimate structures that look exactly like them.

```bash
graphfaker generate supply_chain --scale 0.01 --hardness medium --seed 42 --out ./chain
```

```python
from graphfaker.domains.supply_chain import generate, hardness_report, evaluate

run = generate(scale=0.01, hardness="medium", seed=42)
print(hardness_report(run).summary())
```

## Why the legitimate twins are the point

Almost nothing in procurement data is suspicious on its own. An invoice is a number. A late shipment is weather. A small order is a small order. Every shape worth investigating has an innocent twin that leaves the same trace, and a dataset containing only the guilty version rewards exactly the detector that makes a procurement team's life miserable.

So each play here is paired with a legitimate structure, generated by the same machinery, labelled `is_fraud=False`, and counted as a false positive when a detector flags it.

| play | its legitimate twin | what both look like |
|---|---|---|
| `phantom_supplier` | `disruption_cascade` | invoices with no goods behind them |
| `invoice_kiting` | `consignment_loop` | value going round a circle of related suppliers |
| `split_orders` | `blanket_calloffs` | many purchases just under the approval limit |
| `counterfeit_injection` | `quality_incident` | a lane priced below what the part costs |

A port closure and a shell company produce the same ledger. A framework agreement and a split-order scheme produce the same histogram. That is not a flaw in the data, it is the job.

## What it contains

| node type | attributes |
|---|---|
| Supplier | name, tier (1 to 3), country, capacity, on-time rate, quality score, payment terms, onboarded at, region, category |
| Plant | name, country, capacity, shifts, region |
| Warehouse | name, country, capacity in pallets, automated, region |
| Customer | name, segment, annual spend, region |
| Product | name, family, unit cost, lead time class, hazardous, category |
| Carrier | name, mode, on-time rate, cost per km, region |

| relationship | meaning |
|---|---|
| `SUPPLIES` | supplier to plant: the contract, with its promised lead time and price index |
| `SUBCONTRACTS` | supplier to supplier: the tier structure, where visibility runs out |
| `PRODUCES` | plant to product |
| `STOCKS` | warehouse to product, with reorder point and safety stock |
| `SERVES` | warehouse to customer |
| `HAULS` | carrier to warehouse: the lane, with transit days |

| event channel | meaning |
|---|---|
| `ORDERS` | plant to supplier: purchase orders, scheduled replenishment or ad hoc |
| `SHIPS` | supplier to plant: goods arriving against an order, early, late or partial |
| `INVOICES` | supplier to plant: billing, usually behind the goods |
| `INTERCOMPANY` | supplier to supplier: movements inside a group, in both directions |
| `DELIVERS` | warehouse to customer: the outbound half of the network |

Every event carries `event_id`, `timestamp`, `amount`, `quantity`, a `reference` to the event it follows, and whether it was `scheduled`. **No node or edge attribute says whether anything is part of a pattern.** That lives in `run.truth`, which `--blind` leaves out.

## The ordinary rhythm

The patterns are only as hard as the background they hide in, so most of the work is in the background:

- **Replenishment is scheduled.** A contract reorders weekly, fortnightly or monthly and keeps to it; about 62% of orders come from a schedule. An order off-schedule means something because most are not.
- **Ad hoc demand follows the calendar.** Weekdays over weekends, and the last days of a quarter over everything, because budgets. A cluster of purchases at quarter end is normal, which is exactly why it is a good place to hide one.
- **Lead times are relative to a promise.** A lane delivers around the contracted number, lognormal, worse where the region is exposed. Late means late against its own contract.
- **Amounts follow the part and the lane.** Quantity times unit cost times the region's cost index, and about a fifth of orders are negotiated round figures, because real procurement is full of them.
- **The ledger does not reconcile perfectly.** About 7% of invoices have no shipment behind them (services, tooling, goods invoiced on acceptance) and about 5% of shipments are still unbilled when the period ends.

That last one exists because of a bug we found with the hardness report: when every legitimate invoice had goods behind it, an invoice without them was proof of fraud, and the phantom supplier play was a one-line query.

## What hardness does

`hardness` is an input to a measurement, not a claim. The report scores every feature a procurement analyst would reach for by the AUC it achieves separating a play's suppliers from the suppliers that trade and are in nothing. Measured at `scale=0.03`, five seeds, mean of each play's best single feature (the spread across seeds is about 0.01 for the mean and up to 0.05 for a single play, so read the columns and not the third decimal):

| play | low | medium | high |
|---|---|---|---|
| `phantom_supplier` | 1.000 | 0.948 | 0.937 |
| `split_orders` | 0.935 | 0.945 | 0.941 |
| `counterfeit_injection` | 0.782 | 0.718 | 0.698 |
| `invoice_kiting` | 0.694 | 0.682 | 0.680 |
| **mean** | **0.853** | **0.823** | **0.814** |

At `low` a phantom supplier bills round amounts from an account opened last week, a split-order scheme sits at 99% of the approval limit, and a ring's extra movements are added on top of its members' ordinary trading so their volume stands out. At `high` the amounts are drawn from the legitimate distribution for that supplier's own product, the scheme sits at 70% of the limit, and the ordinary trading is displaced while the pattern runs so the totals say nothing. What is left is the shape: invoices with no goods behind them, a circle that closes, a lane priced under cost.

`split_orders` is flat at about 0.94 across the dial, and that is the honest reading rather than a target we hit. Its signature is mechanical: several orders of similar size to one supplier in a short window. Moving them further under the threshold stops a band rule firing and starts a different feature working, so the play stays about as findable however it is dressed. It is the most detectable thing here and the one a procurement team would in fact catch.

### Camouflage, in this pack, is displacement

The fraud pack strips a mule account's ordinary activity, because an account that only ever forwards money is a real thing and a loud one. Borrowing that here was wrong, and the measurement said so: a supplier stripped of its business is not hiding, it is a differently odd thing, and it made `low` hardness *harder* than `high` for both structural plays.

A supplier is a company with a business. So `activity_camouflage` now controls displacement: a member's ordinary traffic is thinned while its pattern runs, by up to 90% at `high` and not at all at `low`, because a company running a scheme is doing that instead of part of its ordinary work rather than on top of all of it. Only a shell opened to receive invoices has no business at all.

The report also scores each twin against the play it imitates. Lower is better: a decoy separable by one feature is decoration, not a hard negative.

## Detectors, scored

The first rule anyone writes is a threshold count: suppliers with four or more orders just under the approval limit. At `scale=0.01`, `medium`, seed 5:

| | value |
|---|---|
| suppliers flagged | 40 |
| precision | 0.100 |
| recall | 0.174 |
| `split_orders` recall | 1.000 |
| `phantom_supplier` recall | 0.333 |
| `invoice_kiting` recall | 0.000 |
| `counterfeit_injection` recall | 0.000 |
| legitimate suppliers flagged | 33.3% |

It finds every scheme it was written for, misses the two structural plays completely, and accuses a third of the suppliers running perfectly ordinary blanket agreements. The last row is the deployment cost, and it is why `evaluate` reports it separately from precision.

```python
from graphfaker.domains.supply_chain import evaluate

result = evaluate(run, flagged_suppliers=my_detector(run))
print(result.summary())
```

`evaluate` scores at supplier, event and pattern level, with `recall_by_play` so a one-signal detector is visible as one.

## Options

| option | default | meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `scale` | 0.001 | 1.0 is about 50,000 suppliers, 500 plants, 500,000 customers and 50M events |
| `hardness` | `medium` | `low` / `medium` / `high` |
| `approval_threshold` | 25,000 | the signature limit split orders stay under; about 11% of ordinary orders are above it |
| `period_start`, `period_days` | 2026-01-01, 180 | the window |
| `regions` | 6 | latent regions: demand, cost index, exposure to delay |
| `categories` | 8 | latent product families: unit cost, base lead time |
| `patterns` | derived from scale | override patterns per play |

The floors matter at small scales: a network always has at least three plants, four warehouses and two of every play, because a supply chain with one plant is not one.

## Loading and training

The dataset is Parquet with its ground truth, so everything else works unchanged:

```bash
graphfaker load duckdb ./chain     # 291 checks, including the truth subgraph
graphfaker load ladybug ./chain
graphfaker generate supply_chain --scale 0.01 --seed 42 --sink pyg --out ./chain
```

In a graph database the truth lands the way it does for every domain: `Pattern` nodes and `IN_PATTERN` relationships, carrying this domain's own words (`play`, `is_fraud`, `role`). The PyG export gives Supplier `y`, `decoy` and stratified splits, and `y` plus `edge_time` on the five event channels.
